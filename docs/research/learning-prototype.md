# Learning prototype (Python, PIG validation only)

A Python port of the engine's features and decoder with a structured-perceptron trainer, used to
settle the design of the learned weights before the TypeScript trainer existed. Everything below
is measured on the 20 **PIG validation** pieces drawn from the official train split, or by 5-fold
cross-validation over all 120 train pieces. The PIG test split (001-030) was never decoded,
scored or opened. Section 6 lists the settings the engine adopted.

Code: `research/learn/` (Python 3.11, numpy; runs in `research/.venv`). Results: JSON under
`research/learn/results/`, tables regenerated by `python -I -m learn.experiments.make_report`
(research venv interpreter; `results/report_tables.md`, `results/summary.json`).

## 3. Data facts

* PIG train minus validation: 100 pieces, 200 hands, 29,563 notes; validation: 20 pieces, 40 hands,
  8,936 notes (annotations 1-4 per piece).
* In 24 of the 159 train files an annotator puts some notes in the other hand than the first
  annotation. Such notes are gold `0` for that annotation (copying the other hand's finger would be
  wrong).
* 28.3% of hand-annotations (90 of 318 on the 120 train pieces) contain a fingering the solver
  cannot produce under its hard rules: 20 within-slice order violations, 108 adjacent same-finger
  notes while the first still sounds (overlap median 49 ms; 52% at most 50 ms, 35% over 200 ms),
  363 same-finger reuses 3+ notes later while the earlier note still sounds (`heldConflicts`).
  About 1% of notes are involved, so the ceiling cost is small, but a "skip infeasible targets"
  rule throws away a quarter of the hands.
* Mutopia test half: 62 parts, 8,805 fingered notes (single editor, so M_gen = M_high = M_soft =
  M_rec). The Clementi block (71 parts, one Mutopia id) landed in the train half; the split is
  lumpy by construction (224 vs 62 parts for a 70/30 split of ids).
* Beringer: 83 pieces after excluding the C-sharp minor broken chords, 166 hands, 8,696 notes.
  Textbook: 672 entries, 9,216 notes. Mutopia train: 224 parts, 428 hands with at least one
  fingered note, 129,167 notes, 27,378 fingered.

## 4. Method

* Features: `cost(path) = sum_rule weight[rule] x points[rule]`, points summed over the unary,
  pair and triple terms exactly as `solveHand` charges them (template bonus included; the
  "same finger in a slice of more than five notes" slot was free then, the engine now prices it
  with the `doubledFinger` rule, a 100th id). Feature sets are whole families; the base set is
  `rules + colour + trans + chord + lattice + timing` = 99 ids.
* Decoder: exact second-order Viterbi (25 states, 125 triple evaluations per note), the engine's
  tie-breaking (lexicographically smallest optimal path).
* Trainer, per hand example and epoch (seeded shuffle): pick the target annotation (`latent` = the
  one agreeing most with the current plain decode; `first` = lowest slot); target path `y+` =
  gold on fingered notes, model's choice elsewhere (pinned soft decode); most violating path
  `y^` = hard decode with `-rho` on every wrong finger of a fingered note; `loss = max(0,
  w.Phi(y+) - w.Phi(y^) + rho x hamming)`; `w -= tau x sample_weight x (Phi(y+) - Phi(y^))` with
  `tau = min(C, loss / |d|^2)` (PA-I) or `eta` (perceptron); penalty rules clamped at 0; running
  average over all steps; the epoch with the best validation M_gen (averaged weights) is kept.
* Scoring: `fingering_eval.metrics` in `official` mode (the paper's Table 2 scorer), macro over
  pieces; gold notes with finger 0 masked; paired bootstrap over pieces for every delta.
* Protocol: hyperparameters on the 20 validation pieces (section 5.1), then every configuration
  question re-asked by 5-fold CV over all 120 train pieces with a fixed 3 epochs and the last
  epoch's averaged weights (section 5.2), because 20 pieces give ±3.5-point intervals.

mixed in (67.7 vs 70.4), because scale and arpeggio fingerings are far more regular than music.

## 6. Settings adopted by the engine

The TypeScript trainer (`packages/engine/src/learn/perceptron.ts`, run by `digitus2 learn` in `packages/engine/cli/learn.ts`) uses the settings this study supports. Only what is still true of that code is listed.

**Model and trainer**

- `cost(path) = w · Φ(path)`. `pathFeatures` computes Φ with the terms `solveHand` charges: the unary, pair and triple terms and the `template` bonus (`template: -1` per note that takes its template finger). Every decode in the trainer therefore gets the same template matches (`matchTemplates` over `detectRuns`). One finger on two notes two apart in a slice of at most five notes carries no triple terms (the solver charges the chord-order penalty alone there). Penalties (pins, forbidden fingers, the held-note and chord laws) are not weighted terms, so `w · Φ` equals the decoded cost only for a path that pays none.
- `tr:<g><f>:<dir>` also fires when g = f (same-finger hops); their learned weights are among the largest. `shiftFast` is a term between different slices only.
- Averaged passive-aggressive (PA-I) structured perceptron with Hamming-augmented decoding: `C` 0.2 (anything in 0.01-1 works), `rho` 1, 12 epochs, Parncutt initialisation, penalty rules projected to non-negative weights. Averaging is mandatory: without it the validation curve oscillates and loses about 2 points. PA-I against a fixed-step averaged perceptron (eta 0.005-0.1) is a wash.
- `repeatedNoteFingerChange` is not a penalty rule. Its learned weight is negative (experts change fingers on slow repeated notes), so `isPenaltyRule` leaves it out of the projection.
- `template` stays frozen at its Parncutt value 2. Learned freely it fell to 0.4 in the PIG-only set, which would break the textbook gate. Every example has sample weight 1: corpus weights made no measurable difference.
- Targets. With several annotations the target is the one closest to the current plain decode (latent). A note an annotation leaves unfingered has gold 0 (unknown) and adds nothing to the loss or the augmentation. The target path is the pinned soft decode. An example whose target breaks a hard rule is kept, not skipped: 28.3% of expert hand-annotations do (mostly legato overlaps, median 49 ms), and skipping them costs 0.6 points and a quarter of the data. The trainer counts them as `infeasibleTargets`.
- Epoch selection uses the official M_gen of the averaged weights on the validation pieces (`evaluateExamples` returns `{ gen, soft, notes }`, `soft` being the share of notes that match any annotation). On PIG the peak is at epoch 2-5 and the averaged weights then drift down by about a point.
- `digitus2 learn --seeds n` (default 3) trains once per seed (20261013, 20261014, ...) and writes the run with the median validation M_gen. The standard deviation of a run was about 0.4 in the prototype, so a change is credited only above that noise; later hypotheses were accepted at +1.0 on the mean of three seeds.
- The hold tolerance of the held-note law is a parameter (`holdEpsSec`, default 1 ms) of the solver, the trainer and lint. A 50 ms tolerance (H1 in `docs/experiments.md`) shrinks the hard-rule ceiling but gained only +0.93, so 1 ms stays.

**Data**

- Validation: the 20 PIG pieces of `research/data/splits/pig_validation.json` (listed in section 1), read by the engine as `PIG_VALIDATION_PIECES` and not recomputed. They are never trained on; they select the epoch and the seed and fit the confidence temperature. The PIG test split (001-030) is never tuned on.
- Mutopia split: `research/data/splits/mutopia_split.json`, written by `research/learn/make_mutopia_split.py` and reproduced byte for byte from the manifest by a pytest (112 train and 48 test ids, 224 and 62 parts; section 1). The test half is a held-out oracle, and the TypeScript loader reads the file.
- Hands: when annotation k puts a note in the other hand than annotation 1 (section 3), that note's gold in annotation k is 0, never the other hand's finger (`annotationOn`). Beringer contributes 83 pieces and the textbook oracle 672 entries.

**Configurations and licences**

- `pig` is PIG train minus the validation pieces and nothing else: textbook and Beringer mixed in cost PIG accuracy at every weight tried (section 5.4). PIG-fitted weights are research artefacts. `learn` writes them under `research/weights/` (gitignored) and refuses to write under `packages/` or `apps/`.
- `open` is the textbook oracle, Beringer and the Mutopia train half. It is the shipped set `learned-open` (`RECOMMENDED_WEIGHT_SET`). In the prototype it reaches rule-level accuracy on PIG validation (64.9 against 64.7) and gains 8.9 points on the Mutopia test half (65.5 against 56.6). Beating FingerFlow's 68.6 would need PIG-fitted weights.
- Trained by the engine's own trainer (`template` frozen), the shipped set scores 67.75 on the PIG validation pieces (pooled by note; provenance of `weights-learned-open.json`), 63.89 M_gen on the PIG test split and 65.48 on the Mutopia test half (`benchmarks/floor.json`). The measured results of the engine's sets are in `docs/experiments.md` and `docs/research/hard-errors-m3.md`.
- Floors are set per weight set: `benchmarks/floor.json` gates `learned-open`, with its Mutopia floor taken from the open set. The test numbers of the PIG-fitted set are research only.

## 7. Limitations

- The PIG test split was never decoded, so nothing here is a test-split number. The 68.6 of FingerFlow in `baselines.md` is on the test split and is comparable only in spirit.
- The port matches the engine as it was when parity was checked. Later rule changes (for example the `doubledFinger` rule and the `holdEpsSec` parameter) are not in the Python port, and the rules-only score of these pieces has risen since (69.06 with the current rules in `hard-errors-m3.md`, against 64.67 here). Compare configurations within this report, not its absolute numbers with later ones. If `rules.ts`, `solve.ts` or `templates.ts` change, re-run `engine_solve.ts` and `compare_engine.py` against the hashes in `results/engine_parity.json`.
- Parity was checked with the Parncutt weights (integer and half-integer costs, where tie-breaking is exact). With learned non-integer weights, floating-point summation order can break exact ties differently in TypeScript and numpy; expect rare one-note differences.
- The Mutopia split is lumpy: Clementi's 71 parts are all in train, and 224 against 62 parts is a 70/30 split of ids, not of parts. A split by composer or a stratified split would be more balanced; the committed split is kept so that both sides read the same file.
